01

Introduction to Science & Psychology

What is science? Critical thinking. The history of psychology's big ideas.

What is science?

Science has one goal: to create reasonable explanations (theories) that describe reality. The key word is "reasonable" — science doesn't claim to find absolute truth, it claims to get progressively closer to it. Evidence is gathered and evaluated according to rigid, systematically applied rules. That's what separates science from opinion.

Scientific theory ≠ a guess
In everyday language "theory" means a hunch. In science it means a well-supported explanatory framework. It must describe a large class of observations AND make testable predictions about future ones. One contradicting observation can disprove it (Hawking).
Evidence-based
Scientific theory must be grounded in evidence — not intuition, common sense, media reports, or your neighbour's anecdote. The source of knowledge matters as much as the knowledge itself.

Exam content: Be able to explain what makes knowledge "scientific." The answer is always three things: it must be empirical, falsifiable, and verifiable. You'll almost certainly need to define or apply these.

The 6 principles of critical thinking

Critical thinking is the tool you use to evaluate whether research is trustworthy. Not taking things at face value. These 6 principles give you a structured way to assess any claim:

  • 1
    Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence The more a claim contradicts established knowledge, the higher the burden of proof. "Alcohol treats depression" needs very strong evidence before you'd act on it.
  • 2
    Testability (falsifiability) A claim must be testable — it must be possible to prove it wrong. If there's no conceivable test that could disprove it, it's not science.
  • 3
    Occam's razor When two explanations account for the same evidence equally well, prefer the simpler one. Don't add unnecessary complexity.
  • 4
    Replicability Results must be reproducible by other researchers in other settings. A one-off finding proves nothing. Be suspicious of "one study found..."
  • 5
    Ruling out rival hypotheses Before accepting an explanation, you must consider and eliminate other possible explanations. Good research design does this systematically (e.g., control groups).
  • 6
    Correlation ≠ causation Two things happening together doesn't mean one caused the other. Ice cream sales and drowning rates both peak in summer — ice cream doesn't cause drowning.

Exam content: Know all 6 principles by name and be able to give an example of each. Also know the 5 critical thinking questions below — these sometimes appear as short-answer prompts.

  • What am I being asked to believe or accept?
  • What evidence is there to support this?
  • Are there alternative ways to interpret the evidence?
  • What additional evidence is needed to evaluate the alternatives?
  • What conclusions are most reliable?

Historical tour — psychology's major schools of thought

Understanding where psychology came from helps you understand why science is so central to it now. The field has progressively moved toward more empirically rigorous approaches:

Structuralism & Functionalism
Early attempts to study the mind. Structuralism focused on the structure of conscious experience; Functionalism on what the mind does and why (influenced by Darwin).
Psychoanalysis (Freud)
Unconscious processes drive behaviour. Symptoms are symbolic representations of underlying trauma. Important historically but difficult to test empirically — a key limitation.
Behaviourism
Only observable, measurable behaviour counts. Skinner, Thorndike. This was a scientific turning point — it forced psychology to focus on what could actually be measured.
Current approaches
Biological, Cognitive, Humanistic, Neuropsychological, Behaviourism. Modern psychology draws on multiple frameworks depending on the presentation and the research question.
Module 1 Summary

The entire unit rests on one idea: to be an effective psychologist, you must be science-literate. Module 1 establishes what science is and gives you the critical thinking tools to engage with it. The historical tour shows how psychology evolved toward empirical rigour — and why psychoanalysis, despite its cultural impact, sits awkwardly in that story.

Key takeaway: psychology distinguishes scientific evidence from fiction and myths. Your job as a future practitioner is to do the same.

02

Science & Psychology Part 2

Empiricism. Scientific theory. Verifiability. The scientific method. The scientist-practitioner model.

Empiricism

Empiricism is the practice of relying on observation, evidence, and empirical data. But observation alone isn't sufficient — it must be systematic. You need a structured, consistent method of observing, so that what you find reflects reality rather than your own biases or randomness.

Psychology became empirical when Skinner and Thorndike ran controlled experiments on observable behaviour. This is also why operationalising variables matters — turning fuzzy concepts like "wellbeing" into measurable things. Example: the DASS-42 (Depression, Anxiety & Stress Scale) turns mental health into something measurable on a spectrum from normal to severe.

Exam content: Know what empiricism means and why "systematic" observation matters. Know what operationalising a variable means — and why it's necessary for empirical research. Be able to contrast observable behaviours (easy to operationalise) with psychological constructs (harder).

Scientific theory — in more depth

Stephen Hawking on scientific theory

"A theory is a good theory if it satisfies two requirements: It must accurately describe a large class of observations on the basis of a model which contains only a few arbitrary elements, and it must make definite predictions about the results of future observations... you can never prove it. No matter how many times the results of experiments agree with some theory, you can never be sure that the next time the result will not contradict the theory. On the other hand, you can disprove a theory by finding even a single observation which disagrees with the predictions of the theory."

This quote captures two crucial points: (1) theories are always provisional — never "proven," just not yet disproven; and (2) falsification works asymmetrically — you can never fully confirm, but you can fully disprove with one contradictory observation.

Verifiability

Scientific knowledge must be publicly verifiable — made available for criticism, extension, and application by others. It can't just sit in a researcher's desk drawer. As Stanovich (2010) put it: "Knowledge does not exist at all until it has been submitted to the scientific community for criticism and empirical testing by others."

In practice, this means: peer review → publication → replication. All three are the minimum standard. In 4th year, you may actually experience this — having your raw data checked by a supervisor to ensure nothing has been manipulated.

Exam content: Be able to explain why verifiability matters. Connect it to practice: before using an intervention with a client, you need to know the results have been replicated across multiple studies. That's how you identify "gold standard" treatments.

Why psychoanalysis is a useful example

Freud's theories — unconscious processes, psychosexual stages, free association — are genuinely difficult to test empirically and therefore difficult to falsify. This doesn't mean Freud contributed nothing (he brought psychology into popular culture, highlighted childhood trauma, and drew attention to unconscious processes), but it does mean psychoanalytic theories can't be validated as scientific knowledge in the same way that, say, CBT outcomes can.

The scientific method

The scientific method is a cyclical process — not a checklist you complete once:

Step 1: Propose a theory
Based on clinical experience or past literature. This is your starting point.
Step 2: Derive predictions
What would you expect to observe if the theory is correct? These become your hypotheses.
Step 3: Test predictions
Collect empirical data using falsifiable hypotheses. Verify the results through replication.
Step 4: Modify the theory
Theory is supported, modified, or rejected based on findings — then the loop begins again.

The scientist-practitioner model

This is the most important concept in the entire unit. The model holds that science and practice are in a reciprocal relationship:

  • Science informs practice — evidence tells you which interventions work
  • Practice informs science — what you see in clients generates new research questions

You are not just a consumer of research. You are a participant in the ongoing scientific project of understanding human psychology. This is what "scientist-practitioner" means.

Exam content: The scientist-practitioner model almost certainly appears on the exam. Know the definition, know it's reciprocal (bidirectional), and be able to give an example: e.g., a clinician notices that clients with X presentation don't respond well to Y treatment → this observation drives new research → new research produces a better treatment → that treatment improves practice.

Module 2 Summary

Science requires knowledge to be empirical (systematically observed), falsifiable (testable), and verifiable (replicable and publicly scrutinised). Scientific theory is not a guess — it's a provisional, evidence-backed explanation that generates testable predictions.

The scientist-practitioner model is the central model of the entire unit: science and practice feed each other in a continuous loop. You must be literate in both.

Pseudoscience & Psychomythology

Psychomyths, their sources, heuristics, and how to debunk them.

What are psychomyths?

Psychomyths are false beliefs about psychology that are believed by a large proportion of the population. They typically arise from misinterpretation of research or selective attention — we over-attend to dramatic, unusual information and ignore the mundane evidence that contradicts it.

The "10% of the brain" myth — a worked example

Often attributed to William James (1907), who actually wrote about people being "half awake" to their potential — a comment about attention and engagement, not literal brain capacity. This was misread across decades and became the myth that we only use 10% of our brain. From a research perspective, the claim is also untestable as stated. The correct classification: false, not just a myth.

Exam content: Be able to describe what psychomyths are and why they exist. Know the vaccine-autism example in depth — it's used as the unit's central case study. Know the 9 sources of psychomyths listed below.

9 sources of psychomyths

1. Word of mouth
Like the telephone game — meaning shifts and distorts with each retelling.
2. Selective memory
We remember the hits (times something worked) and forget the misses (times it didn't).
3. Causation from correlation
Two things co-occur and we assume one caused the other.
4. Post hoc, ergo propter hoc
"After this, therefore because of this." A classic logical error — timing doesn't imply causation.
5. Biased samples
Generalising from a non-representative group to everyone.
6. Reasoning by representativeness
Judging likelihood by how much something matches our mental prototype.
7. Media portrayals
"Studies show..." used without any actual study. Pop psychology (Dr Phil, A Current Affair) breeds myths through sensationalism.
8. Partial truth
A kernel of real finding, massively overstated or stripped of context.
9. Terminology
Scientific terms get adopted into everyday language with distorted meanings (e.g., "theory" being read as "guess").

Heuristics — why smart people believe myths

Heuristics are mental shortcuts — fast, efficient rules of thumb that help us make decisions without expending a lot of cognitive effort. They're not pseudoscience; they're real cognitive processes. They evolved because they're often useful. But they lead to systematic errors and cognitive biases, which is exactly why psychomyths persist.

Pohl (2004) — three categories
Thinking heuristics
Shortcuts in how we reason and draw conclusions
Judgement heuristics
Shortcuts in how we evaluate and make decisions
Memory heuristics
Shortcuts in what we recall and how we recall it

The specific heuristics — know all of these

Conjunction fallacy
We judge a specific, detailed scenario as more likely than a general one because it "fits" better. Classic example: "Linda is a bank teller AND feminist activist" rated more likely than "Linda is a bank teller" — logically impossible.
Confirmation bias
We seek, favour, and remember information that confirms existing beliefs, and ignore contradictory evidence. Clinical example: GP refers client with "anxiety" → practitioner only notices symptoms that confirm anxiety, misses PTSD.
Illusory correlation
Seeing a relationship between unrelated things. "Lucky socks" working at footy. Clinical example: Assuming all 14–16 year old females present with eating disorders.
Fundamental attribution error
Over-emphasising personality in explaining behaviour, dismissing situational context. Example: Athlete who misses a goal blames themselves, ignoring the wind, the opposing team, the goalkeeper's skill.
Self-serving bias
Good outcomes = my ability. Bad outcomes = external factors. HD on assignment = "I'm smart." Poor mark = "tutor didn't explain it well."
Availability / vividness effect
If something is easy to recall, we assume it's more common or likely. Vivid, dramatic events are more memorable. Media coverage inflates perceived risk. Example: Overestimating danger of bungee jumping because the accidents are what get reported.
Representativeness heuristic
Judging likelihood by how much something matches a mental prototype. Clinical example: If a client "looks like" a perpetrator of family violence (tattoos, imposing), you might unconsciously treat them as guilty — which damages therapeutic rapport.
Forer (Barnum) effect
People rate vague, general personality descriptions as highly accurate and uniquely personal to them. Why horoscopes feel so specific. The basis of much pseudoscience and "cold reading."
Misinformation effect (Loftus & Palmer, 1974)
Post-event information contaminates memory. Changing "hit" to "smashed" in a question about a car crash made participants misremember the speed as higher and falsely recall broken glass. Memory is reconstructive, not a recording.

Exam content (high probability): This module is dense with exam material. You need to:

  • Define heuristics and Pohl's three categories
  • Name, define, and give examples of all heuristics listed above
  • Apply heuristics to the vaccine-autism myth (e.g. confirmation bias: parents who believe vaccines cause autism only notice evidence supporting this view; availability: one memorable child's story outweighs thousands of data points)
  • Apply heuristics to clinical practice scenarios (confirmation bias and representativeness are flagged as most problematic in practice)
  • Describe the misinformation effect and the Loftus & Palmer study
Module 3 Summary

Psychomyths are false psychological beliefs held widely. They arise from 9 identifiable sources including media portrayals, word of mouth, and biased reasoning. Heuristics — mental shortcuts — explain why people maintain these beliefs even when presented with contradictory evidence.

Key lesson: whether a relationship exists between two things (like vaccines and autism) is not a matter of opinion — it is a question for science. And science has answered it clearly. The myth persists not because the evidence is ambiguous, but because of heuristics like confirmation bias and the availability effect.

Professional Practice — General Registration

What psychologists actually do. Training requirements. Core competencies. Self-care.

Types of registration in Australia

General registration
Full registration. Can practice independently. Clients can access Medicare rebates. This is the license that legitimises your work.
Provisional registration
During the supervised training / internship period. You're registered but not yet fully independent.
Non-practising registration
Registered but not currently providing psychological services.

Training pathways to general registration

The 4+2 pathway is being phased out. By the time you graduate, there will be two main options:

5+1 pathway
4 years undergraduate + 1 year honours (or integrated 4+1) = 5 years of study, then 1 year of supervised internship. Internship involves logbooks, case studies, and 300 hours of supervised practice in Semester 2.
Postgraduate Masters pathway
4 years undergraduate + postgraduate masters in an area of practice. Required for endorsement areas like Clinical, Forensic, Neuropsychology etc. This is the longer road but leads to specialist endorsement.

The 8 core competencies for general registration

Every general psychologist must demonstrate these. Science underlies all of them — even the ones that seem purely interpersonal:

1. Knowledge of the discipline
Understanding of psychological theory, research, and evidence base.
2. Ethical, legal & professional matters
APS Code of Ethics, legal obligations, professional conduct standards.
3. Psychological assessment & measurement
Valid, reliable assessment tools. Understanding psychometrics.
4. Intervention strategies
Evidence-based treatments. You must know why something works — i.e., that a significant body of research supports it.
5. Research & evaluation
Ability to engage with, critically appraise, and apply research.
6. Communication & interpersonal skills
Effective communication with clients, colleagues, and in reports.
7. Working in cross-cultural context
Cultural competence. Awareness of how culture shapes presentation and treatment.
8. Practice across the lifespan
Ability to work with clients at different developmental stages.

Exam content: Know all 8 competencies by name. Be able to explain how science relates to at least one or two of them. The module specifically works through Competency 4 (intervention strategies) as an example: you must use evidence-based/"gold standard" treatments, and you know they're gold standard because research has demonstrated their effectiveness.

The psychologist's "promise"

Registration functions like a licence. You cannot practice psychology without it, just as you cannot drive without a driver's licence. This licence represents a promise to society — that your level of training, skills, and competency is sufficient to help people in serious distress. The Medicare rebate system is the government formally endorsing this promise.

This is also why knowing the limits of your competence is so central to ethics. If you're trained in individual therapy and a couple presents, you must be honest that you may not have the skillset for that — rather than proceeding anyway and risking harm.

Module 4 Summary

Psychologists in Australia must be registered with AHPRA. The main training pathway by the time you graduate will be the 5+1 (5 years study + 1 year supervised internship) or a postgraduate masters. General registration requires demonstrating 8 core competencies, all of which are underpinned by scientific literacy.

Registration is a promise: that you are equipped to help people who are genuinely suffering. That promise requires you to stay current with research and to know your limits.

Endorsement Areas — Part 1

Clinical psychology. Counselling psychology. Neuropsychology.

There are 9 endorsement areas in psychology in Australia. Modules 5, 6, and 7 cover all of them. Endorsement requires postgraduate training beyond general registration. The key exam skill is knowing which psychologist is most appropriate for a given client — not just which one could see them.

Exam content: Case study questions about endorsement areas are common. The key logic is: multiple psychologists could technically work with a client, but one is most appropriate based on the presenting concern, context, and the specialist's training. Know the distinct focus of each area.

Clinical

Clinical Psychologist

Assesses and treats diagnosable mental illnesses — schizophrenia, severe depression, complex PTSD, eating disorders. Has specialised training for diagnosis, not just treatment. Uses CBT, DBT, schema therapy, exposure therapy etc. Key distinction: generally registered psychologists can treat but not diagnose.

Counselling

Counselling Psychologist

Specialises in interpersonal, relational, and emotional concerns — communication breakdowns, relationship issues, life transitions, grief. Person-centred approach. Less focused on diagnosable illness; more on wellbeing, adjustment, and quality of life.

Neuropsychology

Neuropsychologist

Brain–behaviour relationships. Cognitive assessment after brain injury, stroke, dementia, or developmental conditions. Highly technical — requires specific training in neuropsychological testing and brain function. Most commonly works in hospital or rehabilitation settings.

Case study logic — worked example

Case: Young person presenting with psychotic symptoms

A generally registered psychologist could see this person and provide support. An Ed & Dev psychologist could also be relevant given the client's age. A health psychologist could address wellbeing components. But a clinical psychologist is most appropriate because: (a) the symptoms are clinically significant indicators of a diagnosable mental illness, and (b) clinical psychologists have specialised training in assessing and treating diagnosable disorders. Diagnosis and targeted treatment come first.

Module 5 Summary

Clinical psychologists are the go-to for diagnosable mental illness. Counselling psychologists specialise in relational and interpersonal wellbeing. Neuropsychologists work with brain–behaviour relationships. When multiple types of psychologist could work with a client, the most appropriate is determined by the primary presenting concern and the specialist's core training.

06

Endorsement Areas — Part 2

Organisational psychology. Community psychology. Forensic psychology.

Forensic

Forensic Psychologist

Works at the intersection of psychology and the legal/criminal justice system. Risk assessments of offenders, expert witness testimony, working with perpetrators and victims in criminal, civil, and family law contexts. Key skills: legal knowledge, court report writing, risk assessment tools.

Organisational

Organisational Psychologist

Applies psychology to workplaces — leadership development, team dynamics, organisational culture, selection and recruitment, management training. Works at both individual and system levels. Relevant when the primary concern is work performance, culture, or leadership.

Community

Community Psychologist

Systemic and social justice focus. Works with communities, organisations, and policy — not primarily with individuals. Concerned with empowerment, human rights, social determinants of health, and structural change. Think NGO leader or policy advisor, not private practice.

Case study logic — Module 6 worked examples

Forensic example

A client involved in family court proceedings needs a psychological assessment and may need someone to provide expert witness testimony. A general psychologist could provide general support. But the forensic psychologist is most appropriate because they have specialist training in court contexts, legal processes, risk assessment, and writing reports for legal purposes.

Counselling vs Organisational

A man presents because poor communication with his partner is affecting his mood at work. His primary concern is the relationship. A counselling psychologist is most appropriate — relational and interpersonal concerns are their core specialty. If instead the concern was about managing a team or leadership effectiveness at a systemic level, an organisational psychologist would be more appropriate.

Module 6 Summary

Forensic psychologists work at the legal system interface. Organisational psychologists work with workplace systems and individuals within them. Community psychologists work at the societal and systemic level. The key differentiator for community psychology is the shift away from the individual toward the system and society.

07

Endorsement Areas — Part 3

Educational & Developmental psychology. Sport & Exercise psychology. Health psychology.

Ed & Dev

Educational & Developmental Psychologist

Works with children, adolescents, and families around learning, developmental challenges, and adjustment. ASD, ADHD, giftedness, school-related distress, parent–child relationships. Strong lifespan focus with emphasis on youth. Key settings: schools, early intervention, private practice.

Sport & Exercise

Sport & Exercise Psychologist

Performance psychology with athletes. Performance anxiety, mental preparation, team cohesion, transition out of sport. Key distinguishing factor: the psychological concerns exist in the context of sport. If anxiety is the issue but sport is the context, sport psych trumps clinical psych.

Health

Health Psychologist

Psychological factors in physical health — chronic pain, addiction, sleep problems, eating issues, anxiety contributing to physical illness. Also works at population level: identifying health behaviours, modifiable risk factors, and disease distribution. Bridge between psychology and medicine.

The "most appropriate" decision — full case study logic

Case: Athlete with anxiety about performance

A clinical psychologist could treat anxiety (it's a diagnosable condition). An Ed & Dev psychologist might be relevant given the client's age. A counselling psychologist could support general wellbeing. But sport & exercise psychologist is most appropriate because the anxiety exists specifically in the context of sporting performance — which is their core specialty.

Exam content: Across Modules 5–7 you need to know all 9 endorsement areas. The exam likely presents a case study and asks you to identify the most appropriate type of psychologist with a rationale. Remember: "most appropriate" ≠ "only one who could see them." Look at the primary presenting concern and match it to who specialises in exactly that.

Modules 5–7 Summary — All 9 endorsement areas

Clinical: diagnosable mental illness. Counselling: relational & interpersonal. Neuropsychology: brain–behaviour. Forensic: legal system. Organisational: workplace & systems. Community: societal & structural. Ed & Dev: child/adolescent development & learning. Sport & Exercise: performance in sporting context. Health: psychology–physical health interface.

Alternative Careers — Research

Research career pathways. Roles for psychology graduates outside registration.

Not everyone becomes a registered psychologist

A Bachelor of Psychology opens doors beyond clinical practice. Two main alternative tracks:

Research careers
Research assistant → PhD candidate → postdoctoral researcher → academic/senior researcher. Honours (4th year) is the gateway. Roles include running participants, data analysis, writing, grant applications, and eventually leading your own research program.
Non-psychology roles
HR management, policy, social work, education, health administration. Psychology skills (research literacy, communication, understanding human behaviour, data analysis) transfer broadly. The unit uses an HR Manager job ad to show how psych competencies map onto non-psych roles.

The research career pathway

Research career stages typically move from: Research Assistant (RA) → Honours → PhD candidate → Postdoctoral researcher → Research Fellow / Associate Professor / Professor. Each stage requires the previous one. The PhD is where you develop specialist expertise and make an original contribution to knowledge.

Module 8 Summary

Psychology graduates aren't limited to registration. Research careers build directly on the scientific skills developed in your degree. Non-psych careers in HR, policy, education, and health also benefit from psychology's core skills — research literacy, understanding behaviour, communication, and evidence-based decision-making.

Ethics in Psychological Practice & Research

Professional ethics. APS Code. Research ethics. The Nuremberg Code. Five principles. Informed consent.

Why ethics in research? — the historical reason

Research ethics became formalised after catastrophic abuses of power in the name of science. Two key examples you need to know:

Nazi experiments (WWII)
Dr Josef Mengele conducted "experiments" on concentration camp prisoners without consent, causing severe harm and death. This directly led to the Nuremberg Code — the first international standard for ethical research.
Tuskegee Syphilis Study
600 poor African-American men were injected with syphilis and studied for 40 years without being told what they had, without receiving treatment (even after penicillin became available), and without true consent. Many died; partners and children were infected.

These examples show why ethical standards in research are not bureaucratic box-ticking — they exist because, historically, without them, researchers harmed people catastrophically.

Exam content: Know both historical examples and what they led to (Nuremberg Code, HRECs, NHMRC guidelines). Be able to explain why each example violated specific ethical principles (Tuskegee violated autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence, and justice simultaneously).

Two types of ethics in professional practice

Virtue ethics
Concerned with the moral character of the practitioner — being honest, courageous, honourable. Gives guidance on how to live and practice, but doesn't provide specific rules for resolving dilemmas. When the code runs out, virtue ethics kicks in.
Principle-based ethics (APS Code)
Clear rules, specific duties, and codes of behaviour. Provides certainty. But can't cover every situation — especially novel ones like social media, AI, or unexpected scenarios (e.g., matching with a client on Tinder).

The 5 basic principles of research ethics

🔓

Autonomy

Participants have the right to make informed, voluntary decisions. Operationalised through informed consent — participants must clearly understand what they're agreeing to, the risks, benefits, and their right to withdraw.

🛡️

Non-maleficence

Do no harm. Research must not damage participants. Risks must be minimised and justified by the potential benefits. The first obligation: don't make things worse.

✅

Beneficence

Research should produce meaningful benefit — for participants, for the field, or for society. Not just "interesting" — actually worthwhile given the risks and resources involved.

⚖️

Justice

Fairness in who bears the risks and who benefits. Tuskegee was a justice violation — a vulnerable population bore all the risk; the benefits went elsewhere. Research burdens and benefits should be distributed equitably.

🔬

Fidelity & Scientific Integrity

Research must be conducted honestly and reported accurately. Violations: falsifying data (Cyril Burt), selective reporting (only publishing positive findings — publication bias), plagiarism, drawing conclusions the data don't support.

Exam content (very high probability): Know all 5 principles by name and definition. Be able to explain how the Tuskegee study violated multiple principles simultaneously. Be able to explain the difference between virtue ethics and principle-based ethics and when each applies.

Professional integrity in practice — the key rules

  • Most ethical problems arise from momentary lapses in judgement — "most of the time" isn't good enough
  • Know the limits of your competence — if you're not trained to do something, say so
  • Psychologists occupy a privileged, powerful position — clients share things they share with no one else. That power dynamic demands extra vigilance
  • The APS Code is a guide, not a complete rule book — novel situations require judgement
The two case studies — know both

Practitioner A: Was "learning as she went" on assessments she wasn't trained in. Clients were getting misdiagnoses. Verdict: unprofessional. You cannot practice outside your competence. You must seek training first.

Practitioner B: Chronically late for sessions, dismissed a single mother's complaint about waiting with "you don't have a job, what difference does it make?" Verdict: unprofessional. Shows complete disrespect for the client and her circumstances.

Limits of the APS Code of Ethics

The code cannot cover every situation. Examples of where it runs out:

  • The Mental Health Act — what do you do when a client refuses treatment but their life is at risk? (MHA can force treatment as a last resort, e.g., nasogastric feeding in severe anorexia)
  • New cultural phenomena — social media, Tinder, AI — the code may not yet address these
  • Vagueness across diverse practice areas — a code that covers private practice AND forensic work AND research will sometimes be imprecisely worded
Module 10 Summary

Ethics in both practice and research are grounded in the same core principles: autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence, justice, and scientific integrity. Research ethics emerged specifically because, without formal constraints, researchers historically caused serious harm.

In practice, ethics means knowing your limits, acting in the client's best interest, and maintaining integrity even when the APS Code doesn't provide a clear answer. Most ethical failures are lapses, not malice — which is why constant vigilance matters.

Working with Diverse Populations — Psychology & Culture

Culture. Enculturation. Cross-cultural psychology. Acculturation. Multiculturalism.

What is culture — and what it isn't

Overt culture
Readily observable and concrete — language, dress, food, customs, rituals, observable behaviour.
Covert culture
Inferred, invisible — values, beliefs, worldviews, assumptions about how the world works. The deeper layer, and often more influential.
Culture ≠ nationality, race, or ethnicity

Nationality is a political construct. Race refers to physical make-up. Ethnicity refers to origins. Culture is related to all three but is distinct — it's the meanings, practices, and values that give structure to experience within a group. These distinctions matter in psychological practice because conflating them leads to stereotyping and misattribution.

Enculturation

The process through which we absorb our culture — learning its norms, values, and behaviours, usually unconsciously, through family, school, peers, media, and social institutions. You don't choose your enculturation — it shapes you before you're aware of it. This has direct implications for how clients understand their own experience and what "help" means to them.

Exam content: Know the definition of enculturation and what factors contribute to it. Know the three theoretical frameworks below (absolutism, relativism, universalism) and be able to differentiate them. Know the emic vs etic distinction.

Three frameworks for cross-cultural psychology

Absolutism
Psychological phenomena are the same across all cultures — culture is background noise. Universal principles apply everywhere. Criticism: ignores real and meaningful cultural differences in how phenomena are experienced and expressed.
Relativism
Behaviour can only be understood within its specific cultural context. No universal principles — everything must be studied from within. Criticism: makes meaningful cross-cultural comparison impossible and risks implying cultures can't be evaluated.
Universalism (the middle ground)
Some psychological processes are universal (e.g., attachment, emotion), but culture shapes how they are expressed, experienced, and interpreted. Most researchers sit here. The approach that best informs ethical, culturally sensitive practice.

Emic vs etic approaches

Emic approach
Study behaviour from the inside — as a member or near-member of the culture. Deep cultural understanding. The "native" perspective. Risk: less generalisable; hard to compare across cultures.
Etic approach
Study behaviour from the outside — as an external observer. Allows cross-cultural comparison. Risk: may impose researcher's own cultural assumptions and miss culturally specific meanings.

Acculturation — Berry's 4-fold model

Acculturation describes what happens when a person from one culture lives within another. Berry's model identifies four strategies based on two questions: (1) Do you maintain your original culture? (2) Do you engage with the new culture?

✅ Integration

Maintain original culture AND engage with new one. Generally associated with the best outcomes — sense of identity, belonging, and flexibility.

➡️ Assimilation

Give up original culture, adopt the new one fully. Can work but involves loss of original identity and community.

⬅️ Separation

Maintain original culture, reject or avoid the new one. May preserve identity but limits engagement with broader society.

❌ Marginalisation

Neither maintain original culture nor engage with the new one. Most difficult outcome — associated with isolation, poor mental health outcomes, and loss of community.

Implications for psychological practice

Much of the existing psychological research and many assessment tools (personality tests, standardised measures) were developed in Western cultural contexts. Applying these uncritically to non-Western populations raises significant concerns about validity and cultural bias. As a practitioner, you must be aware of:

  • The cultural assumptions built into your assessment tools
  • The cultural context shaping how your client understands their own experience
  • Whether your intervention approach is appropriate and accessible to someone from a different cultural background
  • The 10-step model for engagement with Indigenous communities — which prioritises relationship, trust, and community consultation before any psychological work begins

Exam content: Know the definitions of culture (overt vs covert), enculturation, the three cross-cultural frameworks, the emic/etic distinction, and Berry's four acculturation strategies. Be able to explain why Western-developed psychological tools may not be valid across cultures.

Module 11 Summary

Culture shapes how people experience, express, and make meaning of psychological phenomena. As a practitioner, cultural competence isn't optional — it's Competency 7 for general registration. You need to understand how culture differs from race, nationality, and ethnicity; how enculturation works; and how acculturation affects the clients you'll work with.

Universalism is the most defensible framework for cross-cultural research — acknowledging both human commonalities and real cultural variation. When working across cultures, be aware of whether your tools and approaches were designed for the population you're using them with.
